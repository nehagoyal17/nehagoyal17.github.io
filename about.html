Before product management, I spent years in sales, competitive strategy, and enterprise software — at a media company, a middleware software firm, and a global telecom infrastructure provider. Those years built chops that PM school doesn't teach. My product career started at AWS, and that context traveled with me into every platform decision that followed.

Chapter 1 — The Formative Years

Building different chops

Before I ever owned a product roadmap, I owned a sales quota. I started my career at Times Group — learning how buyers make decisions, where real friction lives in a sales cycle, and why a compelling pitch doesn't close if the underlying product doesn't deliver. It was the earliest version of a lesson I'd keep relearning: the user's reality is always different from the builder's assumption.

Times Group Started in sales — built commercial instinct, buyer empathy, and an early conviction that products succeed or fail at the point of adoption
TIBCO Led global competitive intelligence strategy — learned how to read markets, position against incumbents, and synthesize signal from noise at an international scale
Ericsson Product strategy for IoT solutions — navigated the complexity of enterprise B2B platforms, multi-stakeholder environments, and the gap between hardware capability and software adoption

Each of these roles built a distinct capability. Sales built commercial instinct — an understanding of what buyers actually value versus what builders think they value. Competitive intelligence built strategic pattern recognition — the ability to see across markets, identify structural shifts, and translate them into product positioning. IoT product strategy built enterprise complexity tolerance — learning to operate in environments where the customer is not one person, the use case is not one workflow, and the integration surface is never as clean as the architecture diagram.

Taken together, these years formed a foundation that most pure-track PM careers don't develop. When my product career started at AWS, I already knew what it felt like to be on the customer side of a complex product, to read markets strategically rather than just reactively, and to navigate enterprise environments where no one use case represents the whole. That context shaped every product decision that followed.

Chapter 2 — The AWS Years

Building for the builder

At AWS, my job was to take what was deeply complex — cloud migration, containerization, application modernization — and make it accessible to an engineer who just wanted to ship. The customer was always the builder. The product was always the abstraction layer between capability and the person who needed it.

Across container migration tooling, application modernization, and developer integrations, every product had the same underlying challenge: the technology was powerful, the customer need was real, and the gap in the middle was friction. Onboarding friction. Mental model friction. Documentation friction. The job was to close that gap — to make the capability invisible so the outcome could appear.

Developer experience is not a polish layer. It is the product. If the abstraction is wrong — if the onboarding has friction, if the mental model doesn't match how builders actually think — the capability underneath doesn't matter. Nobody will use it.

AWS teaches this loudly. It is what made the transition to building enterprise ML and AI platforms feel natural rather than new.

Chapter 3 — Enterprise AI Foundations

The same lens, at a different scale

When I moved to lead ML and AI Platform product at a large travel enterprise, I was building infrastructure that developers would consume — not tools they'd hold in their hands. But the design principle was the same.

The ML engineer running experiments needed the platform to stay out of their way. The ML scientist querying the data warehouse needed to see features without filing a ticket. The business user building an AI assistant needed to go from idea to execution in hours, not sprints. Three completely different builders. Three completely different definitions of "easy." One platform that needed to serve all of them.

Every AI platform decision — API design, abstraction depth, documentation investment, onboarding sequence — was filtered through a single question: which builder does this serve, and does it make things worse for any of the others? The no-code user and the ML engineer are not competing priorities. They are a design constraint. The platform has to hold both.

A platform that only serves your most technical users isn't a platform. It's a tool for the few, paid for by the many. The goal was one set of foundations — and a different surface for every type of builder on top of it.

The bottleneck to enterprise AI is rarely the model. It's the friction between the person who needs a capability and the infrastructure that provides it. Reducing that friction — for every skill level, for every type of builder — was the entire job.

The Through-Line

The platform disappears. The outcome appears.

A developer migrating containers in two commands instead of two weeks. An ML engineer running experiments without waiting on a deployment ticket. A business user building an AI assistant in hours rather than sprints. Different contexts, different problems, different eras — same design principle.

The best infrastructure is invisible. The capability it unlocks is not.

That conviction runs from the sales floor to developer tools to enterprise AI foundations. It is not a framework borrowed from a book. It is the thing every chapter of this career kept teaching.
